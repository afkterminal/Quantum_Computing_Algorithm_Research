Qubit math

Lectures 1–5. A qubit is a vector, a gate is a rotation, and a measurement is a weighted coin flip.

Measurement and the Born rule

The state cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩ sits between the two outcomes. Each measurement returns only 0 or 1, and the state collapses. Over many shots the fractions approach |α|² and |β|².

Try: set θ to 90° and measure once a few times, then 100 times.

Interference in a circuit

Gates act on amplitudes, and amplitudes can be negative. Two Hadamards take |0⟩ through a superposition and back: the two paths to |1⟩ have opposite signs and cancel.

Try: compare H·H with H·Z·H. Click any slot to change its gate.

Circuit slots

Entanglement with H and CNOT

H puts qubit 1 in superposition, then CNOT flips qubit 2 only in the branch where qubit 1 is 1. The result (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 cannot be split into two separate qubit states, so the two outcomes always agree.

Try: turn H off and run again. CNOT alone leaves |00⟩ unchanged.

Bloch sphere & noise

Lectures 5–9. Every single-qubit state is a point on a sphere. Gates rotate it; noise pulls it inward.

Gates as rotations on the Bloch sphere

Any pure state is cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩, one point on a unit sphere. X, Y and Z are half-turns about their axes; H is a half-turn about the diagonal between x and z; S and T are quarter and eighth turns about z.

Try: start at |0⟩ and press H, Z, H. You end at |1⟩.

Drag the sphere to turn it.

Relaxation and dephasing in the density matrix

Diagonal entries of ρ are populations; off-diagonal entries are coherences. T1 pulls the state back to |0⟩, T2 erases the coherences. As the Bloch vector shrinks inside the sphere, the state becomes mixed and tr(ρ²) drops below 1.

Try: start from |+⟩ and make T2 much shorter than T1.

Starting state

Circle size = magnitude of each ρ entry. Drag the sphere to turn it.

Ramsey decay and spin echo

Spins with slightly different frequencies fan out after a π/2 pulse, so their average signal decays: that fast decay is T2*. A π pulse at time τ mirrors every phase, and the fast spins catch the slow ones at 2τ.

Try: run with the echo off, then on. The echo peak is a bit lower than the start because of true, random dephasing.

Driving spin qubits

Lectures 10–16. A static field gives a Z gate; an oscillating field gives X and Y gates. Silicon spin qubits put both to work.

Larmor precession: the Z gate

A static field B splits the energies of spin up and spin down, so the relative phase between |0⟩ and |1⟩ grows at the Larmor frequency and the vector circles the z axis. Stopping after exactly half a turn is a Z gate.

Try: press “Apply Z gate”, then raise B. A stronger field turns faster.

Seen from the top, the electron precesses clockwise. Drag to turn the sphere.

Rabi oscillation: X and Y gates

A weak field oscillating near the Larmor frequency tips the spin. In the frame rotating with the drive it looks like a steady field along x, so the spin circles from |0⟩ to |1⟩ and back. Off resonance the axis tilts and |1⟩ is never fully reached.

Try: switch to the lab frame to see the fast precession that the rotating frame hides.

Silicon readout: spin-to-charge conversion

One spin’s magnetic moment is too small to sense directly. Instead the dot is tuned so that only the higher-energy spin (↓, with B pointing down) can tunnel into the reservoir. That electron leaving, and a spin-↑ electron replacing it, shows as a blip on a nearby charge sensor.

Try: prepare a superposition and read it out several times. Notice the dot always ends in ↑: the same trick initializes the qubit.

Superconducting qubits

Lectures 16–25. A Josephson junction turns an evenly spaced LC oscillator into a usable two-level system you read out with a resonator.

Why a qubit needs a Josephson junction

An LC circuit is a harmonic oscillator: every level is equally spaced, so a drive tuned to 0→1 also drives 1→2, 2→3, and the state leaks up the ladder. The junction’s cosine potential squeezes the upper levels (anharmonicity ≈ −EC), so the drive only reaches 0↔1.

Try: start the drive on the LC circuit, then switch to the transmon and reset.

Circuit

Frequencies assume EC/h = 250 MHz.

From charge qubit to transmon

The Cooper-pair box energy, H = 4EC(n̂ − ng)² − EJ cos φ̂, depends on the gate charge ng. With EJ ≈ EC the bands are steep, so stray charge shifts the qubit frequency unless you sit at the sweet spot ng = ½. Raising EJ/EC flattens the bands: that is the transmon.

Try: turn on charge noise, move ng off ½, then raise EJ/EC toward 50.

Bands are exact numerical solutions in the charge basis, scaled by E₀₁ at ng = ½.

Dispersive readout

The qubit pulls the resonator frequency by ±χ depending on its state. A probe tone at the bare resonator frequency returns with a different phase for |0⟩ and |1⟩, so each shot lands in one of two clouds in the I-Q plane.

Try: raise the noise until the clouds overlap. With κ = 2 MHz, the clouds are farthest apart when 2χ = κ.

Trapped ions

Lectures 27–28. Lasers cool the ¹⁷¹Yb⁺ ions, drive their gates, and read them out by making one state glow.

Doppler cooling

The 369 nm cooling laser is tuned slightly below resonance and shines from both sides. An ion moving toward a beam sees it Doppler-shifted up onto resonance, absorbs a photon, and gets a kick that slows it. Emission goes in random directions, so those kicks average out.

Try: heat the ions, watch them cool, then move the detuning above zero to see heating instead.

Fluorescence readout

The 369 nm readout laser only drives the F = 1 level. An ion in |1⟩ scatters many photons and glows; an ion in |0⟩ stays dark. Counting detected photons against a threshold tells the two states apart.

Try: run 200 shots, then move the threshold to find the best fidelity.

Photon counts are illustrative: about 12 detected when bright, 0.4 when dark.
